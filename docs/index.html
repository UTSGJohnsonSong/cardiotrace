Three estimands, three designs — and a fourth section asking what limits the third: a standardised prevalence series, a counterfactual test of the pandemic, and a prospective cohort of cardiovascular death

One national survey can answer more than one question, but not with one method. This report states each question as a quantity to be estimated, sets out the design that identifies it, and prices the choices that design requires.

Why one dataset needs three designs

Statistical questions come in three kinds, and the same variable can be required in one, forbidden in another, and irrelevant in the third. Fixing which kind of question is being asked is what makes every downstream choice decidable.

The three kinds of question
 DescriptiveCausalPredictive
AsksHow many, and how has it moved? If we changed X, what happens to Y? Given what we know now, who is at risk?
NeedsSurvey weights, standardisation, intervals A causal graph, confounder control, stated assumptions Out-of-sample validation, calibration
Does not needConfounder controlA high AUC A causal story
Fails byReporting raw rates from an ageing population Interpreting every coefficient in one table as an effect Validating on data that leaks into training

The clearest illustration is age. To describe how disease burden moved across 11 survey cycles, age must be removed — otherwise an ageing population looks like a spreading disease. To predict who will die, age must be kept — it is the single strongest predictor available, and a model without it is worthless. The same variable, opposite treatment, and the only thing that decides which is correct is which question is being asked.

The first three analyses below therefore use three different samples. They are not three views of one table.
